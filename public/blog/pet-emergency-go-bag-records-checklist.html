Pet Emergency Go-Bag: What Records, Medications and Supplies to Pack

A pet emergency kit is more than food and a leash. If you have to evacuate quickly, the records that prove vaccination status, identify your pet and explain medications can matter almost as much as the physical supplies.

By YourPetPass Health Records Team · September 14, 2026 · Educational preparedness guidance

The short answer

  • Keep copies of rabies and vaccination records, a medical summary, prescriptions, microchip information and recent pet photos with your emergency supplies.
  • CDC preparedness guidance recommends a two-week supply of food and water for each animal and, when applicable, a two-week supply of medications.
  • Identification matters twice: use current tags and microchip registration, and keep ownership/contact information in your kit.
  • Do not rely on a phone battery or internet connection alone. Keep accessible digital records and a waterproof paper backup for critical documents.
  • Review the kit on a recurring schedule so medication, food, contact information and records do not expire or become outdated.

Direct answer: a useful pet emergency go-bag combines three things: records that identify and medically describe your pet, supplies that can sustain your pet during disruption, and a plan for where you can actually go together. The CDC specifically recommends veterinary records, rabies and vaccination documentation, prescriptions, microchip information, recent photos, food, water, medications, a carrier or leash, and other basic care supplies.

The best emergency record is the one you can produce when your normal vet is closed, your neighborhood is inaccessible and someone else needs to understand your pet quickly.

Start with the records folder

Emergency kits often focus on physical gear, but records solve a different problem: they let an unfamiliar veterinarian, boarding facility, shelter or caregiver understand what your pet needs without relying on memory.

The CDC's pet disaster-kit checklist includes photocopied veterinary records, rabies certificates, vaccination records, a medical summary, prescriptions, recent test results where relevant, proof of ownership or adoption, pet descriptions, recent photographs, microchip information and emergency contact details.

What this means: your emergency records should answer four fast questions: Who is this pet? Who owns them? What medical care or medication do they need? What proof may another facility need before accepting or treating them?

Do you still need paper copies if records are on your phone?

Yes. Digital access is extremely useful, but emergency planning should account for dead batteries, damaged phones, cellular outages and overloaded networks. Ready.gov recommends taking photos of important documents and saving them securely online; CDC also recommends photocopied veterinary records kept in a waterproof container.

The practical answer is redundancy: keep the most important records digitally accessible and place a small paper set in a waterproof pouch inside the go-bag.

Keep the paper set lean: rabies certificate, concise medical/medication summary, microchip information, owner and backup contact information, and one recent photo can cover much of the critical identification and health context without turning your go-bag into a filing cabinet.

Food, water and medication: how much?

CDC's current pet disaster-kit guidance recommends a two-week supply of food for each animal, stored in waterproof containers, and a two-week supply of water for each animal. It also recommends a two-week supply of medications when applicable, plus medication instructions, and a one-month supply of flea, tick and heartworm preventive.

Those are preparedness targets, not a reason to stockpile medication outside veterinary or pharmacy rules. For prescription drugs, ask your veterinarian what emergency reserve is practical and lawful for your pet, and rotate supplies so they remain usable.

The physical go-bag checklist

  • Food in waterproof packaging and feeding instructions
  • Water and non-spill bowls
  • Current medications and written medication instructions
  • Leash, harness and collar with current ID
  • Appropriately sized carrier or crate
  • Bedding, blanket or towel
  • Cat litter and litter box, when applicable
  • Pet first-aid supplies and a pet first-aid reference
  • Cleanup supplies such as waste bags, paper towels and disinfecting supplies
  • A familiar toy or comfort item if space allows

Identification is part of the emergency kit

CDC recommends collars and tags with current contact information and microchipping pets, with the microchip registered and owner contact information kept current. After a disaster, familiar scents and landmarks can change, which can leave pets disoriented and increase the risk of separation.

Ready.gov also recommends keeping a current photo of you and your pet together. That is easy to overlook until you need to demonstrate that a found animal is yours.

Five-minute check: open your microchip registry account, confirm your phone number and address, photograph your pet from both sides, and make sure the ID tag is readable. That is a small task with disproportionate value during a separation.

Plan where the bag is actually going

A packed bag is not an evacuation plan. CDC warns that many evacuation centers do not accept pets and recommends identifying pet-friendly options in advance, including friends or relatives, boarding facilities, animal hospitals or pet-friendly lodging. Service-animal rules are different from ordinary pet policies, so verify the facility and local emergency guidance that applies to your situation.

CDC also recommends a buddy system in case an emergency happens while you are away. A trusted neighbor or nearby contact should know where carriers and supplies are and how to reach you.

A 20-minute emergency-record drill

  1. Open your pet's current record. Check vaccination dates, allergies, medications and veterinarian contact information.
  2. Verify the microchip. Confirm the number and registry contact details.
  3. Create a one-page medical summary. Include ongoing conditions, allergies, medication names and dosing instructions exactly as prescribed.
  4. Save recent photos. Keep clear identification photos plus one of you with your pet.
  5. Print the critical set. Put it in a waterproof pouch.
  6. Check physical supplies. Rotate food, water and medications as needed.
  7. Put carrier and leash where they are reachable. An emergency kit hidden behind storage boxes is not ready.
  8. Confirm two destinations. Choose a primary and backup place that can accommodate your pet.

How often should you review the kit?

There is no single federal rule for a review interval, but the contents themselves tell you when updates are needed. Review after veterinary changes, vaccination updates, medication changes, a move, a new microchip registration, a change in emergency contacts, or whenever food and medications approach expiration.

A simple recurring calendar reminder can catch the mundane failures that make an emergency kit unreliable: a disconnected phone number, an old prescription, a pet whose weight or medication changed, or a carrier that no longer fits.

FAQ

What veterinary records should I put in a pet emergency kit?

CDC recommends veterinary records including rabies and vaccination documentation, a medical summary, prescriptions and relevant recent test results. Add microchip information, recent photos and owner/contact information.

How much pet food and water should an emergency kit contain?

CDC's current pet disaster-kit guidance recommends a two-week supply of food and a two-week supply of water for each animal.

How much medication should I keep for my pet?

CDC recommends a two-week supply when applicable. Prescription availability can be constrained by veterinary, pharmacy and medication-specific rules, so ask your veterinarian how to build a safe emergency reserve for your pet.

Should pet emergency records be digital or paper?

Use both when possible. Digital records are portable and easy to update; paper copies provide resilience when power, devices or connectivity fail. CDC recommends photocopied records in a waterproof container, while Ready.gov encourages secure digital copies of important documents.

Do emergency shelters accept pets?

Not all do. CDC advises planning pet-friendly sheltering or alternatives before an emergency. Confirm local options and keep backup destinations.

What photo should I keep for identification?

Keep clear recent photos of the pet and at least one current photo of you together. Ready.gov specifically recommends a current photo of you and your pet in case you become separated.
