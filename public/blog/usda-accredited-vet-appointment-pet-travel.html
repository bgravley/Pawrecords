What to Bring to a USDA-Accredited Vet Appointment for International Pet Travel

The appointment goes much more smoothly when your veterinarian has your pet's records and your exact trip details before the certificate work begins. Here's the folder to build before you walk in.

By YourPetPass Travel Desk · September 8, 2026 · Educational travel planning

Bring these five things

  • Your pet's complete vaccination records, especially rabies documentation.
  • Microchip or other identification details required by your destination.
  • Your destination country and planned travel date—not just “Europe” or “sometime next month.”
  • Results or records for any destination-required tests, treatments or prior vaccinations.
  • Any import permit or other supporting document your destination requires.

Short answer: For international travel from the United States, your veterinarian needs enough documentation to verify that your pet meets the destination country's current requirements. USDA APHIS specifically tells accredited veterinarians to review prior medical records, confirm identification, vaccinations, tests and treatments, and make sure the correct certificate is issued within the destination's required timeframe.

That means the most useful thing you can do before the appointment is turn scattered records into one clean travel file.

The certificate is the final document. Your pet's underlying records are what allow the veterinarian to complete it accurately.

1. Start with the destination—not the appointment date

International pet travel rules are set by the destination country, and USDA warns that country requirements can change frequently. Before deciding when to schedule certificate work, confirm the current requirements for your exact destination and species.

Some destinations require only a relatively straightforward certificate and vaccination proof. Others may require specific identification, laboratory testing, parasite treatments, permits or carefully sequenced timing.

What this means: Don't book a certificate appointment first and research the rules later. Work backward from the travel date and the destination's current requirements.

2. Bring the actual vaccination records

A list you typed yourself can be useful for organization, but it is not a substitute for the supporting veterinary record when official documentation is required. USDA's guidance says vaccination certificates should be included when vaccination is required by the destination, and veterinarians should obtain prior records when another clinic performed a required vaccination or test.

For each relevant vaccination, keep the original clinic record or certificate available. Make sure names, dates and identification details are consistent across documents.

3. Have your pet's identification information ready

If the destination requires a microchip or another form of identification, the details need to match the health certificate and supporting records. USDA specifically instructs veterinarians to double-check that an animal's description, identification, vaccination, testing and treatment information matches the medical record and supporting documentation.

A one-digit microchip error can create a much bigger problem than it looks like on a screen.

4. Bring test results, treatment records and permits when required

If your destination requires a laboratory test, parasite treatment or import permit, keep that documentation in the same travel folder. USDA lists test results and destination-issued import permits among the supporting materials that may need to accompany a certificate submission.

Don't add documents simply because another traveler needed them. Requirements vary by destination, species and route. Your goal is a complete file for your trip, not the thickest possible file.

5. Give the veterinarian your exact itinerary

Your travel date matters because health certificates and some treatments have strict validity windows. USDA notes that once a certificate is signed, travelers may have only a limited period to obtain endorsement and travel before the destination stops accepting it.

Before booking
Check the destination's current official requirements and identify long-lead tests or waiting periods.
Before the vet visit
Collect prior vaccination, microchip, test and treatment records plus any required permit.
At the visit
Give the veterinarian the exact destination and travel date so the correct certificate and timing can be confirmed.
After issuance
Complete USDA endorsement when required and finish any remaining destination-specific steps.

What happens if your veterinarian doesn't already have the records?

Ask the clinic that administered the vaccination, performed the test or provided the treatment for the underlying record. USDA guidance for accredited veterinarians explicitly says to obtain previous records for a new patient or when another veterinary clinic holds information needed to complete the certificate.

This is one reason portable pet records matter: travel deadlines are a bad time to discover that an old rabies certificate is sitting in a closed clinic's filing system.

Save this before your appointment: Put the destination, travel date, rabies record, microchip information, required test results, treatment records and permits into one digital folder. Keep the originals available when the destination requires paper documents.

One important distinction: USDA rules vs. airline rules

The destination country's entry requirements and an airline's boarding requirements are not the same thing. USDA notes that airlines may impose different or additional requirements. A carrier can tell you what it requires to transport the animal; it does not replace the destination government's import rules.

For the country side of the trip, start with the responsible government authority. For the carrier side, check the airline separately.

Quick questions

Does every veterinarian issue international pet health certificates?

No. When a destination requires USDA endorsement, USDA says the certificate must be issued by a USDA-accredited veterinarian. Accreditation is voluntary and state-specific, so not every veterinarian is accredited.

Can I wait until the week before travel to start?

That can be too late for some destinations. Requirements can include waiting periods, laboratory testing, treatments or permits. Research the destination first and build the timeline backward from your departure date.

Will USDA tell me what my airline requires?

No. USDA distinguishes destination-country requirements from airline requirements and advises travelers to check with the airline for carrier-specific rules.

Can I rely on digital copies?

Digital organization is useful, but the document format accepted at travel depends on the destination. USDA notes that original signed or endorsed paperwork may need to accompany the pet, so confirm the destination's current document rules.
