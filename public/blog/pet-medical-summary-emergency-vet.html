Pet Medical Summary for an Emergency Vet: What to Include

When an unfamiliar veterinarian needs to make decisions quickly, a concise medical summary can turn scattered records and uncertain memories into usable context.

By YourPetPass Health Records Team · September 15, 2026 · Educational record-organization guidance

The short answer

  • A useful pet medical summary identifies the pet and owner, then lists current medications, allergies or prior drug reactions, major diagnoses, important procedures, vaccination status and regular-veterinarian contacts.
  • For every medication, record the exact name, strength, amount given, frequency and why it is used. Include prescription drugs, over-the-counter products and supplements.
  • Keep the summary concise enough to scan quickly, but keep supporting records available behind it.
  • Update the summary whenever a medication, diagnosis, allergy, vaccination or contact detail changes.
  • Do not use a summary as a substitute for veterinary advice or the original medical record; it is a navigation layer that helps another veterinarian understand the history faster.

Direct answer: a pet medical summary for emergency or unfamiliar veterinary care should answer, at a glance: Who is this pet? What conditions matter now? What medications are being given? What allergies or previous drug problems exist? What important history could change treatment? And who can provide the complete record?

The CDC includes a medical summary, prescriptions, vaccination records and other veterinary documentation in its recommended pet disaster kit. FDA guidance also tells owners to bring a complete medication list to veterinary visits and to discuss drug allergies, previous medication problems and serious or chronic health conditions.

The goal is not to compress every vet visit onto one page. It is to surface the facts another veterinary team should not have to discover by accident.

What this means: the summary should be an index, not an archive. Put high-consequence facts on the first page. Keep lab reports, imaging, discharge instructions, vaccination certificates and full visit notes available as supporting records when they are relevant.

The medication section deserves extra attention

Medication lists are easy to get almost right, which is not the same as right. FDA advises owners seeing a new veterinarian to keep and bring a list of everything the animal is taking, including prescription drugs, over-the-counter products and supplements. FDA also recommends discussing serious or chronic conditions and any medication allergies or previous problems.

For each current medication, copy information from the prescription label or veterinarian instructions rather than relying on memory. Include the medication name, strength or concentration, the amount actually given, how often it is given and the reason your pet takes it. If timing matters, note when the most recent dose was given.

What not to put on the first page

More information is not automatically more useful. A years-long chronology of nail trims, routine invoices and every normal lab value can bury the facts that matter now.

  • Do not copy every visit into the summary just because it exists.
  • Do not guess at diagnoses, medication doses or dates. If uncertain, mark the information for verification.
  • Do not replace precise medication instructions with shorthand such as “heart pill twice a day.”
  • Do not delete older records merely because they are not on the summary. Keep the underlying record available.
  • Do not assume a veterinarian can access another clinic's system immediately, especially after hours.

How to build the summary in 15 minutes

  1. Start with identity. Add the pet's identifying details and your current contact information.
  2. Put medications next. Read each current label and record the exact regimen. Add nonprescription products and supplements.
  3. List allergies and reactions. Separate a known allergy or adverse reaction from a medication that simply did not work.
  4. Add active diagnoses. Prioritize chronic and serious conditions that could affect treatment.
  5. Capture major history. Add surgeries, hospitalizations or other events that remain relevant.
  6. Check vaccines and identification. Keep the current rabies certificate and vaccination record accessible with the summary.
  7. Add source contacts. Include the regular clinic and any specialist most likely to hold important records.
  8. Read it like a stranger. Could someone who has never met your pet understand the essentials in under a minute?
A useful test: hand the page to someone who knows your pet but not the medical details. Ask them to find the current medications, allergies, chronic conditions and regular veterinarian. If any answer takes hunting, simplify the page.

When should the summary be updated?

Update it when the underlying facts change, not just on an arbitrary annual date. A new prescription, stopped medication, new diagnosis, newly identified drug reaction, surgery, vaccination, change of veterinarian, move or new phone number can make an old summary misleading.

A periodic review is still useful as a backstop. Compare the summary against current medication labels and the latest veterinary records, and remove medications that are no longer being given rather than leaving them ambiguously listed.

Digital, paper or both?

Both is the resilient choice. Digital records are easier to keep current and carry between clinics. A concise printed copy can still be useful if a phone is dead, damaged or inaccessible. CDC emergency-preparedness guidance recommends photocopied veterinary records in a waterproof container, while also emphasizing the importance of keeping medical and identification information ready.

If you print the summary, add a visible “updated” date. An undated medication list can create uncertainty about whether it describes today's regimen or last year's.

FAQ

What is a pet medical summary?

It is a concise overview of the health information most useful to someone caring for your pet: identity, medications, allergies or prior drug reactions, active conditions, important history, vaccination status and key contacts. It does not replace the complete veterinary record.

Should I include supplements and over-the-counter products?

Yes. FDA advises owners to keep a list that includes over-the-counter products and supplements as well as prescription drugs and bring it to the veterinary office.

Should the summary include every past diagnosis?

No. Prioritize active, serious and chronically relevant conditions, plus past history that could still affect care. Keep the full record available separately.

Should I include my pet's microchip number?

It is useful identifying information, particularly in emergency and disaster records. CDC recommends keeping microchip information, including the number and registry/company details, in a pet disaster kit.

What if I do not know an exact medication dose?

Do not guess. Check the current prescription label or contact the prescribing veterinarian or pharmacy. Mark uncertain information as needing verification.

Can a medical summary replace calling my veterinarian?

No. It organizes history for communication; it does not diagnose, prescribe or replace veterinary care. FDA advises contacting a veterinarian or local emergency animal hospital for a veterinary emergency.
